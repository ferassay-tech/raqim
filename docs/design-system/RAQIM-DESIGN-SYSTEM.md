# The RAQIM Design System

### A Constitutional Document for Product, Brand, and Experience Design

This document is not a style guide. It contains no hex codes, no component code, no implementation detail. Its purpose is to define *why* RAQIM looks, feels, moves, and speaks the way it does — so that every future decision, made by anyone, at any time, can be checked against a single coherent worldview rather than against personal taste. A style guide tells you what to use. This document tells you how to decide what to use when the style guide hasn't anticipated the question yet — which is most of the time.

---

## 1. Brand DNA

### 1.1 What RAQIM Represents

The name رقيم is not decorative. It is drawn from *al-Raqim* — the inscribed tablet in the story of the Companions of the Cave, the record that preserved names and a story across a span of time long enough that they should, by every ordinary rule, have been forgotten. That is the whole thesis of this company in one word: **some things are worth inscribing so that time cannot erase them.**

This matters for design because it means RAQIM is not in the business of "content." It is in the business of *permanence* — taking a woman's inner life (her reflection, her faith, her growth, the way she is trying to raise her children and herself at the same time) and giving it a form dignified enough to last. Every screen, every product, every sentence either serves that act of preservation or it is noise competing with it.

### 1.2 Emotional Identity

The emotional register of RAQIM is **warmth held with composure.** Not the loud excitement of a sale, not the cool detachment of a tech product — something closer to the feeling of a quiet room, a warm drink, and twenty uninterrupted minutes with a book that was clearly made by someone who cared. The emotion we are designing for is *relief*: relief that something in this woman's day is not asking anything more of her than to sit still and receive it.

This is why RAQIM must never feel urgent. Urgency is the emotional opposite of what the brand exists to offer.

### 1.3 Editorial Identity

RAQIM behaves like a small, opinionated publishing house, not a marketplace. A marketplace's job is to show you everything and let you decide. A publishing house's job is to have already decided — to say "this book is worth your limited time, and here is why." Every title carries a point of view; nothing is merely listed. This editorial confidence is itself a form of respect for the reader: it says her time is too valuable to hand her an unfiltered catalog and walk away.

### 1.4 Luxury Positioning

Luxury, here, is not ornamentation and it is not price. It is **the visible evidence of care in a category that usually receives none.** Arabic self-development and spiritual publishing is a category flooded with cheaply produced, disposable content. RAQIM's luxury is the luxury of a manuscript that was edited twice, a cover that was considered rather than templated, a translation that wasn't run through a machine and left unread. The reader should feel, without being told, that more attention went into this than she expected — that is the entire luxury experience. It costs restraint, not decoration.

### 1.5 Brand Personality

If RAQIM were a person, she is the composed older sister or mentor — the one who has been through what you're going through, who speaks to you as an equal, and who never raises her voice to be heard. She is not a guru selling transformation. She is not a peer venting alongside you. She has authority because she is calm, not because she is loud.

### 1.6 Brand Voice

The voice is warm, exact, and unhurried. It addresses the reader directly and specifically as a woman on her own journey — never a generic "user," never a genderless "you" borrowed from software. It prefers a complete, considered sentence to a punchy fragment. It never uses exclamation points to manufacture excitement, never manufactures scarcity ("only 3 left," "offer ends tonight"), and never borrows therapy-speak or hustle-culture jargon. When RAQIM has good news, it says so plainly and lets the news carry its own weight — the voice trusts the reader to feel the right thing without being pushed toward it.

## 2. Design Philosophy

### 2.1 Core Principles

**Restraint is a form of respect.** Every element added to a screen is a withdrawal from a woman's limited attention. Before anything is added, its cost must be justified — not just its visual appeal.

**Craft outranks decoration.** A visual choice earns its place by improving legibility, pacing, or emotional clarity. If its only job is to look nice, it has not yet earned its place.

**The product is the hero.** The book, the words, the reading moment — these are what the design exists to serve. Any interface element that competes with the content for attention has failed at its one job.

**One coherent experience, not two skins.** Arabic and English, mobile and desktop, storefront and dashboard — these are not separate design problems to be solved separately and reconciled later. They are one philosophy expressed in different contexts.

### 2.2 Decision Rules

When two options are both defensible, choose the quieter one. When adding a new visual element, ask first what it costs the reader before asking what it contributes. When a design decision can only be justified by "it looks more premium," treat that as a warning sign, not a green light — premium, for this brand, is felt through care and restraint, not through visual richness. Consistency of tone, sustained over time, matters more than any single moment of novelty.

### 2.3 What We Always Do

We give type room to breathe. We treat warm, low-saturation neutrals as the default field and gold as a rare mark of emphasis, never a wash of color. We use soft, rounded geometry that echoes paper and binding rather than hardware and screens. We design Arabic first, as the primary reading direction it actually is, and let English follow from that foundation rather than the reverse. We ground every decision in real content, in the real language, because a design that only works with placeholder text is a design that doesn't actually work.

### 2.4 What We Never Do

We never reach for a loud, saturated, or neon accent color. We never use urgency or scarcity as a persuasion device, visually or verbally. We never let an icon, an illustration, or a decorative flourish outweigh the typography it's meant to support. We never treat the Arabic experience as a translated afterthought bolted onto an English-first layout. We never stack effects — shadow on shadow, gradient on gradient, competing accent colors — in the name of richness. Richness, for RAQIM, is depth of care, not density of effect.

## 3. UX Philosophy

### 3.1 Reading-First Experience

The product's entire job is to shorten the distance between "she opened the app" and "she is calm and absorbed." Every additional decision point, every additional screen, every additional friction between arrival and reading is a small failure of the product's core purpose. This is not a storefront that happens to sell books — it is a reading experience that happens to have a storefront attached.

### 3.2 Content Hierarchy

Every screen has exactly one dominant idea. Supporting information recedes visually until it's needed. Two elements of equal visual weight competing for the same attention is not balance — it's a decision the design failed to make on the reader's behalf, and now she has to make it herself.

### 3.3 Cognitive Load

The realistic reader of this product is often a mother reading in stolen minutes — waiting for a child, during a nap, between tasks. She is not budgeting sustained, uninterrupted focus. The design's obligation is to minimize the number of decisions required per screen, because every decision spent on navigating the product is a decision not available for the content itself.

### 3.4 Navigation Philosophy

Moving through RAQIM should feel like walking through a small, well-organized personal library, not a department store. A handful of clearly named destinations, each doing one job, is more trustworthy than a comprehensive menu that tries to anticipate every possible need. Depth should be reachable, not omnipresent.

### 3.5 Progressive Disclosure

A first-time visitor and a devoted returning reader do not need the same density of information on the same screen. The product should reveal depth — extended descriptions, reviews, longer excerpts — as a reward for engagement, not as an upfront wall the casual visitor has to get past. Density offered too early reads as clutter; the same density, offered on request, reads as generosity.

### 3.6 Trust Building

Trust is not manufactured with badges, certifications, or stacks of social proof. It is built from the accumulation of small correct details — typography that's actually considered in both languages, translations that read as if they were written rather than converted, no dead links, no placeholder copy left behind. Trust, for this brand, is the reader's private realization that someone cared about the parts she wasn't even looking for. That realization is the entire marketing strategy.

## 4. Visual Language

### 4.1 Typography Philosophy

Typography is the most direct expression of the brand's voice — before a single word is read, its shape has already told the reader whether this is a considered publishing house or a template. A literary display serif signals seriousness and warmth in a way a geometric sans-serif, borrowed from software interfaces, cannot. The pairing of a considered body face for sustained reading with a more characterful display face for moments of arrival lets the product be comfortable for long reading and distinctive at first glance, without asking either face to do both jobs at once.

### 4.2 Arabic Typography

Arabic is the native language of this product, not a localization of an English original, and its typography must be designed as such from the first decision, not adapted after the fact. Arabic script has its own vertical rhythm, its own connective letterforms, its own relationship between line-height and legibility — none of which can be safely inherited from Latin-type assumptions. A layout mirrored from English will always feel like a translation; a layout that originates from Arabic's own reading direction and letterform behavior will feel native, because it is. Line breaks, truncation, and justification must never be allowed to break a word's connective form or strip a diacritic — a broken letterform is a small but real breach of the same care the brand claims to represent everywhere else.

### 4.3 Spatial Rhythm

The intervals between elements should echo the pacing of reading a physical book — the pause at the end of a paragraph, the margin around a page of text. A product that paces itself like a densely packed dashboard, when its actual content is reflective and literary, creates a felt contradiction between what the screen looks like and what it's asking the reader to do.

### 4.4 Layout Rhythm

Sections should alternate between denser, informational moments and more open, editorial ones — the same way a book alternates between chapter text and a title page's quiet. A layout that never varies its density starts to feel like a form; a layout that breathes in and out feels like it was composed, not assembled.

### 4.5 White Space Philosophy

White space is not empty space — it is where reflection happens. It is the visual equivalent of the pause a good storyteller leaves before an important sentence. Removing it in the name of "efficiency" rushes the reader in exactly the way that talking over someone rushes a conversation, and rushing is the one thing this brand cannot afford to do to its reader.

### 4.6 Grid Philosophy

An underlying grid should be felt as calm, confident alignment — never seen as visible engineering. The reader should never consciously notice a grid; she should simply feel that everything is exactly where it ought to be, the way a well-set page of a well-made book never draws attention to its own margins.

### 4.7 Surface Hierarchy

Depth is created through subtle shifts in warm neutral tone — ivory receding to cream receding to a slightly deeper beige — rather than through many discrete, heavily bordered cards. The product should feel like layers of the same warm paper stacked gently on top of one another, not like panels of glass or plastic assembled into a dashboard.

### 4.8 Color Philosophy

The palette's low saturation is a deliberate statement of maturity and permanence over trend. Gold functions the way a gilt edge or a wax seal functions on a real object — a mark that says *this page matters*, reserved for genuine emphasis, never used as a decorative wash across a surface. The softer secondary tones exist to read as considered and feminine without ever tipping into the pastel, juvenile register that would undercut the brand's editorial seriousness.

### 4.9 Border Philosophy

Borders are quiet separators, not containers. A hairline of low contrast is often enough to suggest a boundary; a heavy, high-contrast box around every element makes the page feel like a form built from isolated widgets rather than a single considered composition. Elements should feel like they belong to the same page even when they're visually distinct from one another.

### 4.10 Radius Philosophy

A consistent, moderate softness in every corner echoes the physical softness of paper, cloth, and binding — the tactile world this brand is trying to evoke even in a digital product. Sharp corners would borrow the coldness of hardware and software; exaggerated, bubble-like rounding would borrow a playfulness the brand's editorial seriousness hasn't earned. The correct radius sits deliberately between those two failure modes.

### 4.11 Elevation Philosophy

Shadows should behave like soft daylight through a window, not a hard studio spotlight — diffuse, low-contrast, and used only where something is genuinely and temporarily raised above the page, such as a modal or a floating action. A shadow placed under static content for decoration is a shadow that has lied about what it's for.

### 4.12 Icon Philosophy

Icons are quiet supporting marks, matched in weight to the typography around them — never a primary channel of communication, and never cartoonish in a product this composed. Where a symbol carries real cultural or religious meaning, that meaning must be treated with the same seriousness as the written word, not reduced to generic decorative clip-art.

## 5. Motion Language

### 5.1 Motion Principles

Every motion in the product should have a plausible analogy in the physical world this brand evokes — a page turning, a breath, light shifting — never the mechanical, springy motion of a machine responding to input. If a piece of motion can't be explained by "this is what it would look like if it were physical," it doesn't belong.

### 5.2 Timing Hierarchy

The size of a change should govern the length of its motion. A full page transition, being a significant shift in context, deserves to move slowly and deliberately enough to be felt. A small piece of feedback — a hover, a press — should resolve quickly, the way a small physical gesture resolves quickly in real life. Motion that ignores this hierarchy, treating a page change and a button press with the same timing, feels mechanically indifferent to what actually happened.

### 5.3 Hover Philosophy

A hover state is a gentle acknowledgment that an element is interactive — a slight lift, a small warmth shift — never a jarring inversion of color or an aggressive jump in scale. It should feel like an invitation, not a demand for attention.

### 5.4 Page Transitions

A transition's job is to preserve the reader's sense of place — where she came from, where she's arrived — rather than to perform a hard cut that erases context. The same way turning a physical page doesn't erase your memory of the page before it, a digital transition should carry a thread of continuity forward.

### 5.5 Loading Philosophy

A loading state should feel like anticipation, not stalling. Calm, unobtrusive indication is enough; an aggressive spinner or a jumpy progress bar introduces exactly the anxiety this brand exists to relieve its reader of.

### 5.6 Micro-Interactions

Small moments of delight are reserved for moments that have genuinely earned them — a successful purchase, a note saved, a milestone reached. Sprinkling micro-feedback onto every routine action turns delight into noise; scarcity is what makes a small delightful moment actually register as delightful.

## 6. Component Philosophy

*(This section describes the intent behind each component family. It does not specify appearance, tokens, or implementation.)*

**Buttons** are considered decision points, not interface utilities. A screen should rarely present more than one primary action, because a primary action's visual singularity is what makes it legible as *the* decision rather than *a* decision among many.

**Cards** (for books and articles) behave like a spine glimpsed on a shelf — meant to invite curiosity, not to aggressively sell. Consistent proportions across the whole catalog create the sense of a matched, curated set, the way a real publisher's imprint has a visual family resemblance across its titles.

**Forms and inputs** should feel like filling in a well-made notebook page, not submitting a bureaucratic intake form. Fields are kept to what's genuinely necessary, touch targets are generous, and validation language is gentle and specific rather than alarming — a mistake in a form is a small, ordinary thing, and the design shouldn't treat it as an emergency.

**Navigation components** exist for wayfinding, not for browsing every possibility at once. The primary navigation should never visually compete with the content it exists to lead someone toward.

**Modals and overlays** are used sparingly, because interrupting a reader's attention is a real cost, not a free action. When used, they soften and recede the background rather than snapping to a harsh full-opacity blackout — even an interruption should be handled gently.

**Book and product presentation** always treats the book as the visual protagonist of its own screen. Surrounding interface chrome recedes. Where the product is given dimension or texture, that choice exists to reinforce that these are being treated as real, ownable objects — not disposable files.

**Admin data tables and lists** are permitted a higher information density than the reading experience, because this is a working tool, not a moment of reflection — but density must never come at the cost of scannability. Clear row rhythm and restrained, purposeful color-coding are what make density usable rather than overwhelming.

**Badges and status indicators** communicate through calm, consistent pairings of color and shape. Their job is to inform at a glance, not to alarm — a product that treats every status as urgent has no way left to signal what's actually urgent.

**Notifications and feedback** (toasts, banners, inline messages) behave like a brief, soft tap on the shoulder — present long enough to be noticed, gone before they become a nag. An error message is always honest and specific about what happened; vagueness in a moment of failure is its own small betrayal of the brand's promise of care.

## 7. Dashboard Experience Philosophy

### How the Administrator Should Feel

The person running RAQIM day to day is very likely doing several jobs at once — publisher, editor, customer support, and shopkeeper, often within the same hour. The dashboard is not a showcase; it is the desk she sits at to do real work, and its success is measured by how she *feels* while working, not by how it photographs.

**Clarity first.** The measure of a good admin screen is how quickly she can answer "what needs my attention right now" — not how much information is displayed, but how little effort it takes to find the one thing that matters in this moment.

**Confidence through honesty.** Every action should make its consequence clear before she commits to it, and every outcome — success or failure — should be reported plainly and immediately. A tool that hides its failures, or reports success it hasn't actually earned, breaks the exact trust this entire brand is built on: if RAQIM promises the reader that things are handled with care, the tool the founder uses to do that handling has to be honest with *her* first.

**Speed as a form of respect.** The same respect for limited attention that governs the reading experience applies here, turned inward. An admin flow with unnecessary steps, unclear states, or ambiguous feedback is asking for cognitive effort that could have gone toward the actual work of running a publishing house.

**Calmness as infrastructure, not decoration.** A dashboard that feels like a control panel — badges blinking, colors competing, dense walls of unstructured data — trains its user into a low hum of stress that she will inevitably carry back into her decisions about the product itself. A calm dashboard is not a cosmetic nicety; it is what makes considered, unhurried decisions about the *reader's* experience possible in the first place.

The dashboard is where the brand's promise is tested every single day before a single customer ever sees it. If editing a book's description feels stressful, cluttered, or uncertain, that tension does not stay contained in the admin panel — it leaks, eventually, into the books themselves.
